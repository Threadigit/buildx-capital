# Power & Grid Infrastructure: The constraint is the opportunity

**US Opportunity Map / 01**  
**BuildX Research**  
**September 2026**

The United States is entering a power-capacity cycle it has not had to manage for decades. Electricity demand is rising again, driven by data centres, advanced manufacturing, electrification and population growth in specific regions. The problem is no longer simply how to produce more electricity. It is how to connect, move, transform and reliably deliver it on the timetable new customers require.

That makes the grid more than a regulated utility story. It is an opportunity across equipment manufacturing, transmission upgrades, interconnection, flexible demand and customer-sited infrastructure.

## THE READ

### What changed

For most of the period between 2005 and 2019, US electricity demand was almost flat. The pattern has broken. The Energy Information Administration estimates that demand grew about 1.7% a year between 2020 and 2025, compared with 0.1% annually in the earlier period. Its January 2026 outlook expects electricity use to keep rising through 2027, producing the strongest four-year demand-growth period since 2000.

The growth is concentrated rather than uniform. Data centres and large industrial loads are putting particular pressure on ERCOT, PJM and selected markets in the Midwest, Southeast and Southwest. This matters because electricity is local before it is national: a new load can be economically attractive and still wait years for generation, transmission, substations or transformers.

The supply pipeline is large but slow. Berkeley Lab counted roughly 10,300 projects seeking US grid interconnection at the end of 2024, representing about 1,400 GW of generation and 890 GW of storage. Yet only 13% of the capacity entering queues from 2000 to 2019 had reached commercial operation by the end of 2024. Median development time for projects that did reach operation has more than doubled to over four years.

Reliability planning is therefore tightening. NERC's 2025 assessment projects 224 GW of summer peak-demand growth from 2025 to 2035, while identifying only 71 GW of approved summer resource additions and a much larger set of prospective projects whose delivery remains uncertain. FERC's Order 1920 now requires long-term regional transmission planning and consideration of grid-enhancing technologies, but planning reform does not remove the physical bottlenecks.

### Why it matters

The investable opportunity is not simply “more generation.” A generator without an interconnection, a data centre without firm capacity and a utility without transformers are all versions of the same problem: valuable assets stranded behind a delivery constraint.

The strongest opportunities sit at the points where time-to-power has become economically decisive. They include products and services that shorten equipment lead times, increase the carrying capacity of existing lines, accelerate interconnection work, manage flexible loads or provide reliable power behind the meter.

This is not one national market. Utility procurement rules, regional transmission organisations, state regulation, power prices, resource mix and permitting differ widely. The edge is regional and customer-specific: know where load is growing, who controls the connection, which constraint is binding and who is willing to pay to remove it.

## THE APPLICATION

### 1. Grid equipment and domestic manufacturing

Transformers, switchgear, breakers, conductors, power electronics and substation components have become schedule-critical equipment. DOE reports that distribution-transformer lead times expanded from three to six months in 2019 to 12 to 30 months in 2023; more recent industry reporting points to one-to-two-year lead times for distribution units and as much as three to four years for large transformers. Excessive customisation also fragments production: DOE has identified more than 80,000 distribution-transformer variants in use across the country.

**Entry route:** expand domestic production of high-demand components; standardise product families; build refurbishment, testing and service capacity near utility customers; secure long-lead materials and qualified labour before adding volume.

**Watch:** utility qualification cycles, electrical-steel and copper exposure, trade policy, customer concentration, warranty risk and the temptation to build capacity against temporary orders.

### 2. Capacity from the grid already in place

New transmission corridors can take years. Advanced conductors, dynamic line ratings, power-flow controls and topology optimisation can release capacity faster by upgrading or operating existing infrastructure more intelligently. DOE cites deployments where dynamic line ratings increased capacity and avoided or deferred larger capital projects; advanced reconductoring can materially increase transfer capability without obtaining an entirely new right of way.

**Entry route:** sell measurable capacity, congestion and reliability outcomes to utilities and large customers; pair hardware with engineering, data and long-term monitoring; focus on corridors where congestion costs or interconnection demand make the benefit visible.

**Watch:** conservative procurement, long sales cycles, integration with utility control systems, cyber requirements and regulated incentives that may favour traditional capital expenditure.

### 3. Interconnection as a service layer

The queue is not only a shortage of wires. It is also a shortage of studies, engineering capacity, site control, project readiness and coordination among developers, utilities and grid operators. Projects that arrive with poor data or speculative positions slow the entire process.

**Entry route:** build tools and services for feasibility screening, power-flow analysis, queue intelligence, site selection, engineering documentation and portfolio triage. The product should help customers abandon weak projects earlier and advance credible ones faster.

**Watch:** dependence on public queue data, changing tariff rules, liability for forecasts, utility-specific workflows and software that identifies a problem without helping deliver the physical solution.

### 4. Customer-sited power and flexible load

For data centres, factories, hospitals and logistics facilities, waiting for a conventional grid upgrade can be more expensive than arranging part of the solution directly. Microgrids, storage, demand response, controllable generation and energy-management systems can reduce peak requirements, improve resilience or bridge the period before full interconnection.

**Entry route:** begin with a customer's cost of delay and required reliability level; combine equipment, controls, fuel or energy supply, maintenance and financing into a contracted service; design the asset to cooperate with the grid rather than operate as an isolated substitute.

**Watch:** air permitting, fuel availability, emissions rules, interconnection limits, equipment utilisation and contracts that leave the provider carrying technology or commodity risk without adequate compensation.

### How to position

**Career builders:** develop expertise at the intersection of electrical engineering, utility regulation, manufacturing, project finance, procurement, construction and power-market operations. The premium will accrue to people who can translate load forecasts into equipment, permits and delivered capacity.

**Operators:** choose one binding constraint and one buyer. A transformer manufacturer, interconnection platform and microgrid developer are not the same business. Win by controlling delivery time, qualification and field execution—not by presenting a broad “grid modernisation” story.

**Investors:** underwrite regional exposure, customer contracts, backlog quality and execution capacity. Distinguish durable demand from speculative queue volume. Favour businesses paid for delivered equipment, released capacity or reliable service rather than distant development milestones.

## Bottom line

The United States does not lack demand for electricity or proposals to supply it. It lacks enough timely, deliverable capacity between the two. The opportunity is in removing that constraint—component by component, corridor by corridor and customer by customer.
